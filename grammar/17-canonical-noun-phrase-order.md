# 17 — Canonical Noun Phrase Order

STRING uses one preferred internal order for complex noun phrases.

This chapter makes explicit rules that were previously distributed across several chapters.

## 1. Minimal noun phrase — CONFIRMED

A noun can stand alone.

```text
buk
book / books
```

There is no mandatory article.

## 2. Canonical structure — WORKING STANDARD

The preferred noun-phrase order is:

```text
[SELECTOR] [POSSESSOR] [QUANTITY] [MODIFIER...] NOUN [RELATIVE CLAUSE]
```

Each bracketed component is optional.

The noun is the semantic head.

## 3. Selector — WORKING STANDARD

The selector position can contain a deictic or interrogative selector such as:

- `dis` — this / these;
- `dat` — that / those;
- `wat` — what / which.

Examples:

```text
dis buk
this book / these books

wat buk?
what book? / which book?
```

Only one primary selector is normally needed.

## 4. Possessor — CONFIRMED

The possessor appears before the possessed noun phrase.

Simple form:

```text
mi buk
my book
```

With a selector:

```text
dis mi buk
this book of mine
```

The pronoun does not change into a possessive form.

## 5. Quantity — CONFIRMED

A number or quantifier appears before descriptive modifiers and before the noun.

```text
tiri buk
three books

meni buk
many books
```

The noun itself remains unchanged.

## 6. Descriptive modifiers — CONFIRMED

Descriptive modifiers appear immediately before the noun.

```text
gud buk
good book
```

More than one modifier may be used when the meaning remains clear.

STRING does not add agreement endings.

## 7. Combined example — WORKING STANDARD

The components keep the same order when combined.

```text
dis mi tiri gud buk
these three good books of mine
```

The phrase contains:

```text
dis   mi   tiri   gud   buk
SEL   POSS QUANT  MOD   NOUN
```

No word changes form.

## 8. Each, any, both, same, and other — WORKING STANDARD

Quantity/reference words use the slot that best matches their meaning.

Examples:

```text
hic gud buk
each / every good book

heni gud buk
any good book

bot gud buk
both good books

sem gud buk
the same good book / same good books

hader gud buk
other good book / other good books
```

`sem` and `hader` behave as descriptive/reference modifiers.

## 9. Headless noun phrases — WORKING STANDARD

When the omitted noun is immediately recoverable from context, a selector or quantifier may stand alone.

Examples may include:

```text
dis
this / these

dat
that / those

sam
some

bot
both

hader
the other / others
```

Do not omit the noun when the reference would become unclear.

## 10. Relative clause — CONFIRMED

A relative clause follows the completed noun phrase.

```text
buk dat mi si
book that I see
```

With modifiers:

```text
dis gud buk dat mi si
this good book that I see
```

Relative material does not interrupt the pre-noun modifier sequence.

## 11. Relational phrase after a noun — WORKING STANDARD

An explicit relation introduced by a preposition normally follows the noun phrase it qualifies.

This is useful when a simple pre-noun possessor would become ambiguous.

```text
NOUN PHRASE + hava + RELATED NOUN PHRASE
```

`hava` is therefore available for explicit **of**-type relations rather than forcing arbitrarily long stacks of bare possessors.

## 12. Bare possession stays shallow — WORKING STANDARD

The short possessor-before-noun construction is preferred for one clear possessor.

When possession or association becomes nested, use explicit relational phrases rather than requiring the reader to decode a long chain of adjacent nouns.

This is a clarity rule, not an inflection rule.

## 13. Coordination — CONFIRMED

Complete noun phrases can be joined with `han` or `hor`.

```text
NOUN PHRASE han NOUN PHRASE
NOUN PHRASE hor NOUN PHRASE
```

Each coordinated phrase follows the same internal order.

## 14. No hidden agreement — CONFIRMED

Nothing in the noun phrase changes form because of:

- gender;
- number;
- definiteness;
- grammatical case;
- the person of the possessor.

Order and explicit words carry the meaning.
